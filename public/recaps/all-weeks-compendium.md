# Course Recap: Introduction to Game Theory

The first class introduced game theory as the study of **interactive decision-making**. Unlike an individual decision problem, a strategic decision cannot be evaluated independently because its outcome also depends on what other decision-makers do. A firm considering whether to reduce prices, offer coupons, advertise, charge an additional fee, or invest in technology must therefore anticipate how competitors are likely to respond.

### Basic Elements of a Game

A game can be analyzed by identifying three fundamental elements:

- **Players:** The individuals, firms, or organizations making decisions
- **Strategies:** The actions available to each player
- **Payoffs:** The consequences of each possible combination of actions

Defining these elements correctly is essential. In business applications, payoffs may include not only immediate profits but also market share, customer acquisition, reputation, and future competitive position. Different conclusions about a game may therefore arise because people assign different payoffs to the same outcomes.

### Simultaneous and Sequential Decisions

The class distinguished between **simultaneous-move games** and **sequential-move games**. In a simultaneous-move game, each player chooses without observing the other players’ current choices. The decisions do not have to occur at exactly the same time. The defining feature is that each player acts without knowing what the others have chosen.

In a sequential-move game, one player acts first and another responds after observing that action. The order of decisions can affect the outcome because an early action may influence the choices available to others. This introduces the strategic importance of timing and commitment.

### Best Responses and Dominant Strategies

The class initially focused on one-shot simultaneous-move games. A **best response** is the action that gives a player the highest payoff for a particular action taken by another player. Strategic analysis therefore requires players to consider what they should do under each possible action of their opponent.

A **dominant strategy** is an action that is the player’s best response regardless of what the other player chooses. Although choosing a dominant strategy is individually rational, it does not necessarily lead to the best collective outcome.

### The Prisoner’s Dilemma

The **prisoner’s dilemma** illustrated the conflict between individual incentives and collective welfare. Each player has an incentive to act aggressively or defect, regardless of what the other player does. As a result, both players choose their dominant strategies.

However, the resulting outcome is worse for both players than mutual cooperation would have been. The problem is not simply a lack of understanding or communication. Each player has an individual incentive to deviate from cooperation, even when both recognize its collective benefit.

This example demonstrates an important lesson of game theory: **individually rational decisions can produce a collectively undesirable outcome**.

### Business Applications

Price competition provides a practical example of the prisoner’s dilemma. Two firms may both benefit from maintaining relatively high prices, but each firm has an incentive to reduce its price to attract customers. Once one firm cuts its price, the other may respond in the same way. The result can be a price war in which both firms earn lower profits.

A similar pattern can arise when food-delivery companies offer coupons. Each company may use coupons to attract customers from its competitor. If both offer substantial discounts, neither gains a lasting competitive advantage, while both bear the cost of the promotion.

Other examples discussed in class included:

- **Advertising:** Competing firms may advertise heavily because reducing advertising unilaterally could result in lost visibility or market share. If both advertise aggressively, their relative positions may remain similar while both incur substantial costs.
- **Airline baggage fees:** Whether charging a fee is attractive depends on competitors’ policies and on how customers respond. The analysis therefore requires careful specification of the payoffs.
- **Shipping and promotional policies:** Free shipping or discounts may attract customers when introduced by one company, but the advantage may disappear when competitors imitate the policy.
- **Investment in AI hardware:** Companies may invest heavily not only because of the direct benefits, but also because failing to match competitors’ investments could create a long-term strategic disadvantage.

These examples show why business decisions must be analyzed in relation to competitors’ actions rather than solely on their direct costs and benefits.

### Nash Equilibrium

The class also introduced **Nash equilibrium**. An outcome is a Nash equilibrium when each player’s strategy is a best response to the strategies chosen by the others. No player can improve their payoff by changing their own action alone.

A Nash equilibrium is therefore strategically stable, but it is not necessarily desirable. In the prisoner’s dilemma, mutual defection is a Nash equilibrium because neither player can benefit from changing alone. Nevertheless, both players would be better off under mutual cooperation.

This finding challenges the idea that rational individual choices always produce an efficient collective outcome.

### Cooperation, Self-Restraint, and Repeated Interaction

The class briefly considered how repeated interaction can change strategic incentives. In a one-time game, a player may exploit a short-term opportunity without facing future consequences. When the same players interact repeatedly, aggressive behavior may lead to retaliation, loss of trust, or reduced cooperation later.

The discussion of OPEC illustrated this difficulty. Oil producers may collectively benefit from limiting output, but each producer has an incentive to increase its own production while others exercise restraint. Sustaining cooperation therefore requires discipline, monitoring, and credible consequences for deviation.

This relates to the broader idea of **self-restraint**. Strategic success may require players to resist an immediate gain in order to preserve a more valuable long-term relationship. However, self-restraint is difficult when a player cannot be confident that others will behave similarly.

### Commitment and Unpredictability

The class also introduced two possible strategic tools:

- **Commitment:** A player may influence others by taking an action that is difficult or costly to reverse. A commitment is effective only if other players regard it as credible.
- **Unpredictability:** In some competitive situations, being predictable allows an opponent to choose an effective response. Varying one’s behavior may therefore create a strategic advantage.

These strategies serve different purposes. Commitment makes intended behavior more predictable and credible, while unpredictability makes it more difficult for competitors to respond effectively. Their usefulness depends on the nature of the game.

## Overall Academic Takeaway

Game theory does not guarantee that a player will win. Instead, it provides a structured framework for identifying players, strategies, payoffs, and possible responses. It helps decision-makers understand why rational behavior can generate poor collective outcomes and whether cooperation, repeated interaction, commitment, or changes in incentives could improve the result.

The central lesson of the first class was that **a strategic decision cannot be properly evaluated without considering how other players are likely to respond**.

# Course Recap: Sequential Games and Backward Induction

The second class reviewed strategic interdependence, dominant strategies, and the prisoner’s dilemma before introducing **sequential games**, in which players move in a particular order. The main focus was how the timing of decisions affects strategic behavior and how players can anticipate future responses when making current decisions.

### Review of Strategic Interdependence

The class began by revisiting the idea that a player’s best decision depends on what other players are expected to do. Examples such as price competition illustrated how individually rational choices can produce poor outcomes for everyone.

Although cooperation may improve the collective outcome, it can be difficult to sustain when each player has an incentive to act in their own short-term interest. Trust, shared norms, repeated interaction, and changes in incentives may help players avoid these outcomes.

### Simultaneous and Sequential Games

The class distinguished between two types of games:

- **Simultaneous games:** Players choose their actions without observing the current choices of others.
- **Sequential games:** Players move in turn, and later players observe earlier actions before making their decisions.

In a sequential game, the order of moves matters because an earlier action can influence how another player responds. Players must therefore look ahead and anticipate the consequences of each possible action.

Business examples included product launches, market entry, pricing decisions, lending rates, and production decisions. These examples demonstrated that the value of moving first or waiting depends on the structure of the particular game.

### Representing Sequential Games with Game Trees

Sequential games can be represented using a **game tree**. A game tree identifies:

- the players;
- the order in which they move;
- the actions available at each decision point;
- the possible paths generated by those actions; and
- the payoffs at the end of each path.

Each decision point is represented by a node, while the branches represent the available actions. A finite game tree has a clear beginning and ends at terminal nodes, where the players’ payoffs are specified.

Constructing the game tree requires careful judgment about who moves first, what later players observe, and which actions are realistically available. An incorrectly specified sequence of moves can lead to an incorrect strategic prediction.

### Actions and Strategies

The class also distinguished between an **action** and a **strategy**. An action is a particular choice made at one decision point. A strategy is a complete plan describing what a player would do at every decision point at which they might be called upon to act.

When a player moves only once, the distinction between an action and a strategy may not appear important. When a player may move more than once or may need to respond to different earlier actions, however, a complete strategy must specify the player’s intended response in every possible situation.

### Backward Induction

The main analytical technique introduced in the class was **backward induction**. Rather than beginning at the start of the game and simply predicting what might happen next, backward induction begins with the final decision.

The method involves three steps:

1. Identify the last player required to make a decision.
2. Determine which action that player would choose at each possible final decision point.
3. Work backward through the game tree, using these predicted choices to determine what earlier players should do.

By anticipating how later players will respond, an earlier player can select the action that produces the most favorable eventual outcome. Backward induction therefore connects present decisions with their future strategic consequences.

### Market Entry and the Incumbent’s Response

Market entry was used as a central example. A potential entrant first decides whether to enter a market. If entry occurs, the incumbent firm must decide whether to **fight** the entrant or **accommodate** its entry.

The entrant should not base its decision only on the incumbent’s stated threat to fight. Instead, it should examine the incumbent’s payoffs after entry has already occurred. If accommodating entry would be more profitable than fighting, a rational incumbent would accommodate at that stage.

The entrant can anticipate this response through backward induction. If entry followed by accommodation gives the entrant a favorable payoff, it may choose to enter despite the incumbent’s threat.

This example shows that strategic decisions should be based on what another player will have an incentive to do when the relevant decision point is reached, rather than simply on what that player currently claims it will do.

### Credible and Non-Credible Threats

The market-entry example introduced the importance of **credibility**. A threat can influence another player’s behavior only if carrying it out would be rational when the time comes.

An incumbent may announce that it will respond aggressively to any entrant. However, if fighting is costly and accommodation becomes more profitable after entry, the threat may not be credible. A rational entrant should therefore distinguish between the incumbent’s words and its actual future incentives.

A threat becomes more credible when the player has made a commitment that changes those incentives or makes retreat difficult. Investments in excess capacity, contractual obligations, established policies, and reputational concerns may make a threatened response more believable.

### First-Mover and Second-Mover Advantages

The class examined whether moving first creates a strategic advantage. A first mover may shape the game by making an early commitment, securing customers or resources, or limiting the choices of later players.

However, moving first is not always better. A later mover may observe the first mover’s action, learn from its experience, and adjust accordingly. Waiting may allow a firm to avoid early mistakes, improve its product, or respond more effectively to market conditions.

Whether there is a **first-mover advantage** or **second-mover advantage** therefore depends on the specific payoffs, information, and available responses in the game.

### Technology Launch Timing

The timing of a technology launch provided an application of sequential-game analysis. A firm may choose to launch early or wait until competitors reveal their products and the market provides more information.

Launching early may allow a firm to:

- establish a market position;
- attract users before competitors;
- build a reputation as an innovator; and
- influence subsequent industry development.

Waiting may allow a firm to:

- observe the performance of an early entrant;
- learn from its successes and failures;
- improve the technology before launching; and
- avoid some of the uncertainty and cost of pioneering a new market.

The appropriate choice depends on how competitors are expected to respond and how the benefits of early entry compare with the informational advantages of waiting.

### Platform Entry and Regulatory Responses

The class also applied game-tree analysis to decisions involving platforms and regulators. A platform might choose among entry methods such as organic expansion, acquisition, or partnership, after which a regulator may approve, restrict, or challenge the chosen approach.

To analyze such a situation, the model must clearly identify:

- the platform and regulator as players;
- the sequence of their decisions;
- the entry methods available to the platform;
- the possible regulatory responses; and
- the payoffs associated with each final outcome.

The platform should anticipate how the regulator is likely to respond to each entry method. This expected regulatory response then influences the platform’s initial choice.

### Practical Role of Economic Models

The class emphasized that game trees are simplified representations of business situations. Their purpose is not to reproduce every detail of reality, but to isolate the most important strategic relationships.

A useful model should identify the relevant players, actions, timing, information, and payoffs clearly enough to explain the logic behind a decision. Assumptions and payoff assignments should be justified because different assumptions may generate different predicted outcomes.

## Overall Academic Takeaway

The central lesson of the second class was that **timing changes strategic incentives**. In a sequential game, a player should anticipate how later players will respond before selecting an initial action.

Backward induction provides a systematic method for doing this. It begins with the final decisions, determines what rational players would do at those points, and then works backward to identify the optimal earlier choices.

The analysis also demonstrated that threats are strategically relevant only when they are credible and that moving first is not automatically advantageous. The value of moving early, waiting, committing, fighting, or accommodating depends on the structure and payoffs of the particular game.

# Course Recap: Games with Simultaneous Moves and Nash Equilibrium

The third class in **Strategic Thinking (Game Theory for Business Strategy)** advanced from sequential interactions to **simultaneous-move games**, in which decision-makers must choose their actions without observing the current decisions of their competitors. The session established the formal mathematics of best responses, iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS), the concept of strategic stability through **Nash Equilibrium**, the four archetypal 2x2 games, and the foundational oligopoly models of Cournot (quantity) and Bertrand (price) competition.

---

### 1. The Nature of Simultaneous Moves

In strategic business decision-making, "simultaneous" does not necessarily mean that actions are taken at the exact same second on a clock. Rather, it means that **neither decision-maker can observe the other's choice before making their own commitment**. 

Real-world business environments characterized by simultaneous interaction include:
- **Procurement and Government Tenders:** Sealed-bid auctions for infrastructure contracts or spectrum rights.
- **Retail Holiday Promotions:** E-commerce platforms finalizing their Black Friday discounts without knowing competitors' exact flash-sale markdown tiers.
- **Semiconductor Foundry Allocations:** Semiconductor giants committing annual wafer fab allocations across leading-edge nodes without observing rival fab roadmaps.
- **Platform Hardware Launch Pricing:** Sony and Microsoft setting launch prices for new console generations (PS5 vs. Xbox Series X).

Because decision-makers cannot observe rival actions in real time, they cannot simply react. Instead, each firm must formulate a rational belief (conjecture) about what rivals are likely to do, and optimize against those expectations.

---

### 2. The Normal Form (Payoff Matrix) Representation

A simultaneous-move game is formally characterized by three foundational elements:
1. **Players ($N$):** The set of strategic decision-makers (e.g., Airbus and Boeing).
2. **Strategy Spaces ($S_i$):** The complete set of actions available to each player $i$.
3. **Payoff Functions ($u_i$):** The valuation, profit, or market share that player $i$ receives from every possible combination of chosen strategies.

In a two-player game, this is represented by a **Payoff Matrix (Bimatrix)**, where row choices represent Player 1's strategies, column choices represent Player 2's strategies, and each cell contains the payoff pair $(u_1, u_2)$. By convention, the first number belongs to the Row player, and the second belongs to the Column player.

---

### 3. Best Responses and Dominance

The core analytical building block of simultaneous-move games is the **Best Response Function**, denoted $BR_i(s_{-i})$.

- **Definition of Best Response:** An action $s_i^*$ is player $i$'s best response to rival strategy $s_{-i}$ if and only if:
 $$u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) \quad \forall s_i \in S_i$$
- **Strictly Dominant Strategy:** An action that is a best response to *every* possible strategy the rival might play. If an executive possesses a strictly dominant strategy, they should play it regardless of rival intentions.
- **Strictly Dominated Strategy:** An action for which another strategy always delivers a strictly higher payoff, regardless of what competitors choose.

#### The Law of Rationality:
> **Rational executives never play strictly dominated strategies.**

---

### 4. Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IESDS)

When games feature multiple complex options, decision-makers can simplify the strategic landscape using **Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IESDS)**:
1. Identify and eliminate any action that is strictly dominated for either player.
2. In the resulting smaller game, re-evaluate remaining actions. Previously viable options may now be strictly dominated because the pruned rival actions are no longer credible threats.
3. Repeat the process until no strictly dominated actions remain.

**Key Mathematical Theorem:** IESDS never eliminates any Nash Equilibrium. If IESDS reduces the game to a single outcome, the game is **Dominance Solvable**, and the outcome is the unique, predictable Nash equilibrium.

---

### 5. Nash Equilibrium: Strategic Stability

Named after Nobel laureate John Forbes Nash Jr. (1950), a **Nash Equilibrium** is a strategy profile $(s_1^*, s_2^*, \dots, s_n^*)$ in which every player's strategy is simultaneously a best response to the strategies chosen by everyone else:
$$u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall i, \; \forall s_i \in S_i$$

#### Strategic Characteristics of Nash Equilibrium:
- **No Unilateral Regret:** Given what competitors have done, no firm has an individual incentive to alter its choice.
- **Self-Enforcing:** An equilibrium does not require external legal enforcement; each player's self-interest naturally sustains the outcome.
- **Stability $\ne$ Optimality:** Crucially, a Nash equilibrium guarantees stability, but NOT collective efficiency. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, rational equilibrium produces mutual destruction.

---

### 6. The Four Archetypal 2x2 Simultaneous Games

Executives must instantly recognize four archetypal market structures:

| Game Archetype | Strategic Structure | Corporate Reality | Resolution Mechanism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. The Prisoner's Dilemma** | Strictly dominant defection; unique Pareto-inferior Nash Eq | Price wars, coupon battles, ad spending arms race | Binding contracts, long-term repeated interaction, structural differentiation |
| **2. Coordination Game** | Multiple pure Nash Eq along diagonal; miscoordination is disastrous | Standard wars (NACS vs. CCS, Blu-ray vs. HD-DVD) | Focal points (Schelling points), industry consortia, early commitment |
| **3. Hawk-Dove / Chicken** | Two asymmetric Nash Eq; mutual aggression leads to mutual ruin | Aggressive price wars, capacity flooding, labor strikes | Credible commitment to stand firm, burning bridges, preemption |
| **4. Matching Pennies** | Zero-sum; cyclic best responses; NO pure strategy Nash Eq | Cybersecurity auditing, tax compliance, soccer penalty kicks | Mixed strategies (Week 4): deliberate randomization to maintain unpredictability |

---

### 7. Oligopolistic Competition: Cournot vs. Bertrand

In oligopoly markets, the dimension of simultaneous competition fundamentally determines profit margins and industry structure:

#### Cournot Competition (Quantity / Capacity):
- Firms choose production volumes or capacities $q_1, q_2$ simultaneously.
- Market price adjusts to clear total industry supply $P(Q) = a - b(q_1 + q_2)$.
- Quantities are **strategic substitutes**: when a competitor expands output, your optimal response is to contract.
- Equilibrium price settles strictly between Monopoly and Perfect Competition:
 $$P_{\text{monopoly}} > P_{\text{cournot}} > P_{\text{competitive}} = MC$$
- **Real-World Domain:** Capital-intensive industries with irreversible capacity build-ups (commercial aircraft assembly, semiconductor foundries, petrochemicals).

#### Bertrand Competition (Price):
- Firms choose prices $p_1, p_2$ simultaneously for homogeneous goods.
- Consumers purchase exclusively from the lowest-priced supplier.
- Any firm priced above rival gets 0 demand; undercutting by $0.01 captures 100% of the market.
- **The Bertrand Paradox:** With only two firms, price collapses to marginal cost:
 $$p_1^* = p_2^* = MC, \quad \Pi_1 = \Pi_2 = 0$$
- **Escaping the Bertrand Trap:** High-margin executives escape cutthroat price competition via product differentiation, brand moats, switching costs, capacity caps, or repeated tacit cooperation.

---

### 8. Executive Decision-Making Checklist

Before committing resources in simultaneous competitive markets:
1. **Never play dominated moves:** Audit every project; discard strategies that lose under all rival scenarios.
2. **Anticipate competitor rationality:** Do not rely on competitor incompetence; model their best responses rigorously.
3. **Seek self-enforcing stability:** Partnerships that conflict with private incentives will unravel.
4. **Choose your competitive dimension:** Compete on capacity and differentiation rather than unconstrained price.
5. **Transform the game when trapped:** If stuck in an aggressive Prisoner's Dilemma, redesign payoffs through loyalty programs, exclusive channels, or regulatory standards.

# Course Recap: Mixed Strategies & Strategic Randomisation

The fourth class in **Strategic Thinking (Game Theory for Business Strategy)**, directly addressed the fundamental strategic dilemma: **what should an executive do when no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists?** When deterministic actions create predictable vulnerabilities that competitors can exploit, rational play requires **deliberate strategic randomisation** (Mixed Strategies).

> **Core Maxim:** *"When predictability makes you exploitable, randomisation can be rational."* 

---

### 1. Opening Intuition: From Rock-Paper-Scissors to Cyclic Instability

In sequential games (Week 2), players look ahead and reason backward. In simultaneous games with pure strategies (Week 3), players identify best responses and seek stable cells. But what happens when best responses form an endless cycle?

#### The Rock-Paper-Scissors Dilemma (Slide 3)
- Rock is beaten by Paper; Paper is beaten by Scissors; Scissors is beaten by Rock.
- Always playing Rock is exploitable; always playing Paper is exploitable; always playing Scissors is exploitable.
- **The Only Stable Behavior:** Play each action with probability $\frac{1}{3}$. When the opponent cannot forecast your action, they cannot profit from targeting a counter-move.
- **Insight:** Randomisation is not careless gambling; it is a calculated commitment that **removes the exploitable pattern**.

---

### 2. The Core Lecture Case: Coffee Chain Promotion Timing (Slides 5–13)

To ground mixed strategies in competitive business strategy, the lecture examined a simultaneous scheduling duel between competing coffee chains:

#### The Situation & Payoff Matrix
A coffee chain chooses whether to run its weekly promotion on **Monday** or **Wednesday**. Its main rival simultaneously chooses which day to prepare and staff a defensive counter-campaign:

| Coffee Chain \ Rival | Rival: Prepare Mon ($q$) | Rival: Prepare Wed ($1-q$) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **Promote Monday ($p$)** | $(3, 3)$ | $(6, 0)$ |
| **Promote Wednesday ($1-p$)** | $(5, 1)$ | $(3, 2)$ |

*First payoff: Coffee Chain; Second payoff: Rival.*

#### Economic Logic Behind the Numbers (Slide 6 & 22)
- **Payoff Formula:** $\text{Payoff} = \text{Customer Gross Contribution} - \text{Promotion or Defense Cost}$.
- **Monday Demand Advantage:** Monday baseline consumer foot traffic is substantially higher than Wednesday.
- **Defensive Preparedness:** A prepared defensive campaign blunts the promotion's effectiveness:
 - $\text{Mon–Mon } (3, 3)$: High demand, but the defense limits promotional conversion.
 - $\text{Wed–Wed } (3, 2)$: The defense works, but protects a smaller baseline market opportunity.
- **Wrong-Day Defensive Preparation:** Preparing counter-promotions is fixed and costly:
 - $\text{Mon–Wed } (6, 0)$: Chain captures the large Monday opportunity completely undefended; rival wastes defensive resources.
 - $\text{Wed–Mon } (5, 1)$: Chain gains open traffic on Wednesday, while the rival salvages minimal customers on Monday.

#### Best-Response Cycling (Slide 7)
1. At $[\text{Mon}, \text{Mon}]$, the chain wants to switch to Wednesday to evade defense ($(5, 1)$).
2. At $[\text{Wed}, \text{Mon}]$, the rival wants to shift defense to Wednesday ($(3, 2)$).
3. At $[\text{Wed}, \text{Wed}]$, the chain wants to switch back to Monday to capture peak volume ($(6, 0)$).
4. At $[\text{Mon}, \text{Wed}]$, the rival wants to defend Monday ($(3, 3)$).

> **Conclusion:** No cell has overlapping underlines. **No pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists.**

---

### 3. The Indifference Principle & Equilibrium Derivation (Slides 9–13)

#### Cardinal Utility & Expected Payoffs
An expected payoff is a probability-weighted average of uncertain outcomes:
$$E(u) = \sum_{j} \Pr(j) \cdot u_j$$

#### The Core Principle (Slide 10)
In a fully mixed equilibrium, every pure strategy played with positive probability must yield the **exact same expected payoff**. If one action yielded even a penny more in expectation, a rational player would select that action with 100% certainty, destroying the mixed equilibrium.

> **The Fundamental Rule:** Your mixing probability is determined by equalising the **OTHER** player's payoffs!
> - Find the chain's probability $p$ by equalising the **rival's** expected payoffs.
> - Find the rival's probability $q$ by equalising the **chain's** expected payoffs.

#### Step 1: Solve Chain's Mix $p$ (Slide 11)
Let $p = \Pr(\text{Promote Monday})$. The chain chooses $p$ to make the **rival indifferent** between its two preparation days:
- $E(\text{Rival Prepare Mon}) = 3p + 1(1-p) = 1 + 2p$
- $E(\text{Rival Prepare Wed}) = 0p + 2(1-p) = 2 - 2p$

Equating the rival's payoffs:
$$1 + 2p = 2 - 2p \implies 4p = 1 \implies \mathbf{p^* = 0.25}$$

$$\text{Chain promotes Monday with } 25\%, \text{ Wednesday with } 75\%.$$

#### Step 2: Solve Rival's Mix $q$ (Slide 12)
Let $q = \Pr(\text{Prepare Monday})$. The rival chooses $q$ to make the **chain indifferent** between its two promotion days:
- $E(\text{Chain Promote Mon}) = 3q + 6(1-q) = 6 - 3q$
- $E(\text{Chain Promote Wed}) = 5q + 3(1-q) = 3 + 2q$

Equating the chain's payoffs:
$$6 - 3q = 3 + 2q \implies 5q = 3 \implies \mathbf{q^* = 0.60}$$

$$\text{Rival prepares Monday with } 60\%, \text{ Wednesday with } 40\%.$$

#### Step 3: Verify Expected Payoffs & Strategic Interpretation (Slide 13)
- **Chain's Equilibrium Payoff:** $E[u_{\text{Chain}}] = 6 - 3(0.60) = 5(0.60) + 3(0.40) = \mathbf{4.2}$
- **Rival's Equilibrium Payoff:** $E[u_{\text{Rival}}] = 1 + 2(0.25) = 2 - 2(0.25) = \mathbf{1.5}$

#### The Counter-Intuitive Managerial Insight
- Monday is the **larger baseline market opportunity**, so the rival defends Monday the majority of the time ($q^* = 60\%$).
- Anticipating this heavy defensive barrier, the rational coffee chain **promotes on Wednesday three times more frequently** ($p^* = 25\%$ Mon, $75\%$ Wed) to harvest unguarded demand!

---

### 4. A Reliable 6-Step Mixed-Strategy Workflow (Slide 14)

The lecture laid out a structured algorithmic workflow for solving any two-by-two game:

```
1. Mark pure best responses ──> Confirm no pure equilibrium exists
2. Define variables in words ──> p = Pr(Row Action 1), q = Pr(Col Action 1)
3. Use Column player's payoffs ─> Solve Row's p* (Equalise Col's choices)
4. Use Row player's payoffs ───> Solve Col's q* (Equalise Row's choices)
5. Check probabilities ────────> Confirm 0 <= p*, q* <= 1 and sum to 1
6. Substitute back ────────────> Verify indifference and calculate E(u)
```

#### Common Traps to Avoid (Slide 25)
1. **Never Assume 50–50:** Equilibrium probabilities depend on payoff differentials, not coin flips.
2. **Never Use Your Own Payoffs to Solve Your Probability:** Row mixes to keep Column indifferent; Column mixes to keep Row indifferent.
3. **Never Mix Over Dominated Actions:** Strictly dominated strategies always receive 0 probability.
4. **Indifference Does Not Mean Paralysis:** Being indifferent makes a player willing to randomize; their randomization sustains the opponent's equilibrium behavior.

#### Application 1: Promotion & Product Launch Scheduling
- **Context:** Launching promotions, scheduling flash discounts, allocating digital ad spend, or selecting product reveal windows.
- **Offensive Goal:** Catch competitors off-guard when defenses are dispersed.
- **Defensive Goal:** Anticipate and neutralize offensive customer acquisition.

| :--- | :--- |

### 6. Exit Ticket & Synthesis (Slide 20–21)

1. **A mixed strategy is a formal probability distribution** over pure strategies.
2. **Mixing is relevant whenever deterministic behavior is unstable or exploitable.**
3. **Expected payoff is a probability-weighted average** of cardinal outcomes.
4. **Supported strategies must yield equal maximum expected payoffs.**
5. **Your probability is discovered by equalising the OTHER player's payoffs.**
6. **Equilibrium probabilities reflect underlying demand and cost structures, rarely 50–50.**

> **Exit Ticket Challenge:**
> *"Why is your equilibrium probability calculated using the other player's payoffs? Why must the other player be indifferent?"*
> 
> **Answer:** If your opponent strictly preferred one pure strategy over another ($E[u] > E[u']$), they would play that strategy 100% of the time with certainty. Once they become predictable, your own incentives to randomize would collapse. Therefore, you must calibrate your probability $p^*$ so that the opponent has zero incentive to deviate from their randomized posture.

# Course Recap: Commitment and Strategic Moves

The fifth class in **Strategic Thinking (Game Theory for Business Strategy)**, addressed one of the most powerful concepts in executive decision-making: **how to change the rules of the game before your rivals move**. While Week 4 focused on managing predictability through mixed strategies (making rivals indifferent to avoid exploitation), Week 5 shifts to **restricting flexibility to force a favorable response from competitors**.

> **Core Maxim:** *"Commitment changes the game; cheap talk describes the game you wish existed."* 

---

### 1. From Week 4 to Week 5: Two Strategic Philosophies (Slide 2)

| Dimension | Week 4: Mixed Strategies | Week 5: Strategic Commitment |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Strategic Goal** | Randomise actions; eliminate exploitable patterns | Restrict flexibility; force favorable rival choices |
| **Timing** | Simultaneous decisions | Sequential moves (act *before* the rival) |
| **Incentives** | Make the rival indifferent between alternatives | Change your *own* future incentives so threats/promises are credible |
| **Flexibility** | Preserves optionality across probability distributions | Deliberately destroys or penalises future alternatives |

---

### 2. Opening Dilemma: Can a Threat Deter Entry? (Slides 3, 11–13)

#### The Baseline Entry Game: Why Empty Threats Fail (Slide 11)
Consider a potential market entrant ($E$) deciding whether to **Enter** or **Stay Out** of a lucrative market currently monopolised by an incumbent ($I$). If the entrant enters, the incumbent chooses whether to **Fight** (launch a damaging price or advertising war) or **Accommodate** (share the market as a duopoly).

```
 [ Entrant ]
 / \
 (Enter) (Stay Out)
 / \
 [ Incumbent ] (0, 5) [End]
 / \
(Fight) (Accommodate)
 / \
(-1, -1) (2, 2)
```
*(Payoffs: Entrant, Incumbent)*

1. **Subgame Analysis at the Incumbent's Node:**
 - If entry occurs, the incumbent compares $u_I(\text{Accommodate}) = 2$ against $u_I(\text{Fight}) = -1$.
 - Because $2 > -1$, a rational incumbent **always accommodates**.
2. **Backward Induction by the Entrant:**
 - The entrant looks ahead and anticipates that the incumbent's threat to fight is an **empty bluff**.
 - Comparing $u_E(\text{Enter}) = 2$ against $u_E(\text{Stay Out}) = 0$, the entrant chooses **Enter**.
3. **Equilibrium Outcome:** Entry occurs and is accommodated. The incumbent's pre-entry warning (*"If you enter, we will crush you"*) is pure **cheap talk** with zero deterrent power.

---

### 3. Transforming the Game Through Strategic Commitment (Slides 12–13)

To deter entry, the incumbent must alter its future incentives **before** the entrant moves.

#### The Strategic Commitment Action
Suppose the incumbent pays an upfront capital cost $K = 1$ for dedicated plant capacity or exclusive supplier contracts. This prior commitment alters post-entry operational economics:
- With expanded dedicated capacity, the marginal cost of aggressive production drops, raising the fighting payoff from $-1$ to $1$.
- Accommodation is now less attractive or constrained, yielding $0$.

#### The Altered Post-Commitment Subgame
```
 [ Entrant ]
 / \
 (Enter) (Stay Out)
 / \
 [ Incumbent ] (0, 4) [End: 5 - K=1]
 / \
(Fight) (Accommodate)
 / \
(-1, 0) (2, -1)
[Note: Incumbent net payoffs after K=1: Fight yields 1-1=0; Accommodate yields 0-1=-1]
```

1. **Backward Induction Post-Commitment:**
 - Now, facing entry, the incumbent compares $u_I(\text{Fight}) = 0$ against $u_I(\text{Accommodate}) = -1$.
 - **Fighting is now the dominant, credible response!**
2. **Entrant's Decision:**
 - The entrant foresees that entering leads directly to an incumbent price war ($u_E = -1 < 0$).
 - The rational entrant chooses **Stay Out** ($u_E = 0$).
3. **Incumbent's Net Payoff Comparison (Slide 13):**
 - **Without Commitment:** Entry accommodated $\implies \text{Incumbent Profit} = \mathbf{2}$.
 - **With Commitment:** Entry deterred $\implies \text{Incumbent Profit} = 5 - K = 5 - 1 = \mathbf{4}$.
 - Because $4 > 2$, investing $K = 1$ in the strategic commitment device is an optimal, high-return executive decision.

---

### 4. What Is Strategic Commitment? (Slide 5)

> **Formal Definition:** A strategic commitment is an **observable earlier action** that changes a player's **future incentives or feasible choices** and therefore changes how others respond.

#### The 4 Critical Pillars of Commitment:
1. **Prior:** Executed before the rival's relevant choice point.
2. **Observable:** The rival can clearly verify or reliably infer the commitment.
3. **Irreversible (or Costly to Reverse):** Withdrawal triggers substantial legal, financial, or reputational penalties.
4. **Payoff-Relevant:** Fundamentally reshapes continuation payoffs, constraints, or available actions.

---

### 5. Testing Credibility at the Future Decision Node (Slide 6)

A strategic move is only as strong as its **credibility**. The lecture highlighted the cardinal test:

- **Credible Move:** The promised or threatened action will be **strictly optimal (or unavoidable)** when the future decision node is actually reached.
- **Non-Credible Move:** The player will privately prefer to renege or abandon the announcement once the rival has acted.

$$\text{Credibility Rule: Always evaluate incentives at the FUTURE decision node, NOT at the announcement stage!}$$

---

### 6. Sunk Costs: Important but Often Misunderstood (Slides 8, 24)

Executive teams frequently confuse past expenditures with credible commitments. The lecture unpacked the boundary between the **sunk-cost fallacy** and **true strategic commitments**:

#### The Sunk-Cost Fallacy
*"We have already spent $50M on this project, so we cannot afford to back out now."*
- If an expenditure $K$ is unrecoverable regardless of whether you fight or accommodate in the future, it reduces all continuation payoffs equally ($u - K$).
- In marginal decision-making, common past expenses drop out of the comparison:
 $$(u_{\text{Fight}} - K) > (u_{\text{Accommodate}} - K) \iff u_{\text{Fight}} > u_{\text{Accommodate}}$$
- **A pure historical expense does NOT create credibility.**

#### When Capital Expenditure Becomes a Strategic Commitment
A past expenditure only deters rivals if it **changes future continuation options**:
- Building a specialised semiconductor fab changes future **marginal cost** and **production capacity**.
- Entering a long-term supply agreement with non-refundable minimum take-or-pay clauses adds **penalties for withdrawal**.
- The mechanism is the **changed continuation game**, not the historical expense!

---

### 7. Costly Signalling vs. Strategic Commitment (Slides 7, 27)

| Feature | Costly Signal (Information) | Commitment Device (Incentives/Game) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Mechanism** | Alters rival's **beliefs** about private information, type, or cost structure. | Alters player's **future payoffs, action space, or constraints**. |
| **Key Requirement** | Single-crossing property: costly action must be cheaper or more beneficial for strong types. | Irreversibility: reversing the action is prohibitively costly. |
| **Example** | Heavy brand advertising to signal product durability. | Building excess production capacity or signing take-or-pay contracts. |

### 8. The Four Commitment Devices (Slide 9)

1. **Capacity & Physical Infrastructure:** Expanding factories, warehouses, or dedicated GPU clusters lowers marginal expansion costs and makes aggressive market defense self-enforcing.
2. **Contracts & Financial Penalties:** Most-favored-nation clauses, liquidated damages, or long-term exclusivity contracts lock in commitments through third-party enforcement.
3. **Reputational Bonding & Public Stances:** Visible CEO declarations, public quality guarantees, or policy stances create severe audience and brand costs if abandoned.
4. **Burning Bridges & Cutting Off Retreat:** Deliberately removing exit options (the classic Cortés strategy) leaves fighting as the only available action.

---

### 9. Commitment vs. Flexibility Trade-Off (Slide 17)

Commitment is a double-edged sword: by restricting your own future options, you gain bargaining leverage but forfeit adaptation.

| Commitment Is More Valuable When... | Flexibility Is More Valuable When... |
| :--- | :--- |
| Rival response is highly sensitive to future incentives. | Demand, technological paths, or regulations are volatile. |
| First-mover advantages or scarce resources can be locked in early. | Assets face rapid technological obsolescence. |
| Reversal would destroy executive credibility. | Staged learning, prototyping, and option value are critical. |
| Rivals cannot easily duplicate or bypass the commitment. | Competitors can cheaply bypass or imitate the commitment. |

## 2. Opening Decision: Price Competition Revisited

### The Baseline Stage Game
Two competing firms simultaneously decide whether to **Maintain** price margins or offer a secret **Discount** in each operating period:

| Your Firm \ Rival | Rival: Maintain ($C$) | Rival: Discount ($D$) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **You: Maintain ($C$)** | $(4, 4)$ | $(1, 5)$ |
| **You: Discount ($D$)** | $(5, 1)$ | $(2, 2)$ |

*First payoff: Row Player (Your Firm); Second payoff: Column Player (Rival).*

### Strategic Properties of the One-Shot Interaction:
1. **Dominant Strategy:** For each firm, discounting yields a strictly higher immediate payoff:

2. **Nash Equilibrium:** Both firms play their dominant strategy $[D, D]$, producing an equilibrium payoff of $(2, 2)$.
3. **Collective Inefficiency:** Mutual maintenance $[C, C]$ yields $(4, 4)$—earning each firm double the profit.
4. **The Central Strategic Question:** *Can the prospect of future interaction sustain Maintain–Maintain today without illegal explicit cartels?*

## 4. Why a Known Final Period Unravels Cooperation

Consider repeating the Prisoner's Dilemma for a fixed, publicly known number of rounds $T$ (e.g., a 10-year procurement contract expiring on December 31):

1. **At Period $T$ (The Final Round):**  
   There is no future tomorrow. No punishment can ever be executed. Period $T$ reduces identically to a one-shot game. Both firms' dominant strategy is to **Discount** (payoff $(2, 2)$).
2. **At Period $T - 1$:**  
   Both firms look ahead and recognize that period $T$ will inevitably end in $[D, D]$ regardless of what happens today. Maintaining price at $T - 1$ cannot protect tomorrow’s cooperation. Consequently, the incentive to discount at $T - 1$ is unconstrained. Both firms Discount at $T - 1$.
3. **Backward Induction Unraveling:**  
   The exact same backward-induction logic cascades all the way back to Period 1.

> **The Backward-Induction Theorem:**  
> *In any finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with a known, fixed terminal date, mutual defection repeats in every single period.*

---

## 5. Three Ways to Model the Time Horizon

To discipline opportunistic behavior in the present, players must face an **indefinite horizon** where the game never possesses an obvious "last round":

| Horizon | Formal Meaning | Strategic Implication | Real-World Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Finite, Known End** | Everyone knows the exact terminal period $T$. | Backward induction unravels cooperation completely from period 1. | Lame-duck CEOs, expiring short-term construction joint ventures. |
| **Indefinite Horizon** | The interaction continues to next period with probability $p \in (0, 1)$. | There is never a certain final period; future discipline remains potent in every round. | Ongoing corporate supplier networks, platform partner ecosystems. |
| **Infinite Horizon** | Theoretical benchmark with no terminal period ($t \to \infty$). | Tractable mathematical proxy for ongoing long-term business relationships. | Sovereign debt, global container shipping alliances, duopoly airline routes. |

*Crucial Insight:* The essence of repeated-game cooperation is **not literal immortality**, but rather **the absence of a known final interaction**.

## 6. Two Foundational Repeated-Game Strategies

A repeated-game strategy is a complete plan of action conditioning today's choice on the entire history of past play:

### 1. Grim Trigger (Friedman 1971)
- **Rule:** Begin by cooperating. Continue cooperating as long as nobody has ever defected. If the rival discounts even once, switch to cutthroat competition ($D$) **forever**.
- **Strength:** Creates the maximum possible deterrent penalty, lowering the required patience ($\delta^*$) needed to maintain peace.
- **Weakness:** Exceptionally fragile. A single data error, miscommunication, or external demand shock triggers permanent price war destruction.

### 2. Tit for Tat (Axelrod 1984)
- **Rule:** Begin by cooperating. In every subsequent period, exactly copy the rival's action from the previous period.
- **Strength:** Highly forgiving and transparent. Punishment ceases immediately once the rival resumes cooperation.
- **Weakness:** Vulnerable to echoing retaliation cycles (defects trigger alternating counter-defects).

## 7. The Discount Factor $\delta$: Patience & The Value of the Future

The discount factor $\delta \in [0, 1]$ measures how strongly a decision-maker values future earnings relative to cash in hand today:

$$\delta = \frac{p}{1 + r}$$

Where:
- $p$ is the probability that the business relationship continues into the next period.
- $r$ is the firm's cost of capital / interest rate per period.

### Interpretation:
- A payoff of $4$ received next period is worth $4\delta$ today.
- A payoff of $4$ received two periods later is worth $4\delta^2$ today.
- **High $\delta$ ($\delta \to 1$):** High patience, low interest rates, highly stable and ongoing market relationship $\implies$ **Cooperation is easy**.
- **Low $\delta$ ($\delta \to 0$):** High short-term pressure, quarterly earnings desperation, high risk of disruption $\implies$ **Cooperation collapses**.

---

## 8. Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivations

### Derivation 1: Value of Cooperating Forever ($V_C$)
Under mutual cooperation $[C, C]$, each firm earns $4$ in every period:

$$V_C = 4 + 4\delta + 4\delta^2 + 4\delta^3 + \dots = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} 4\delta^t$$

Using the geometric series formula $\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} a r^t = \frac{a}{1 - r}$ for $|r| < 1$:

$$V_C = \frac{4}{1 - \delta}$$

### Derivation 2: Value of Deviating Once under Grim Trigger ($V_D$)
If your firm chooses to cheat by offering a secret discount in Period 1:
- Today ($t=0$): You capture the unilateral temptation payoff of $5$ (while rival gets $1$).
- Tomorrow onward ($t \ge 1$): The rival observes the discount and triggers permanent retaliatory punishment. Both firms discount forever, yielding $2$ in every continuation period.

$$V_D = 5 + 2\delta + 2\delta^2 + 2\delta^3 + \dots = 5 + \frac{2\delta}{1 - \delta}$$

### Derivation 3: The Critical Threshold $\delta^*$
Cooperation is self-enforcing if and only if the present value of lasting cooperation is at least as large as the value of cheating:

$$V_C \ge V_D \iff \frac{4}{1 - \delta} \ge 5 + \frac{2\delta}{1 - \delta}$$

Multiplying both sides by $(1 - \delta)$:
$$4 \ge 5(1 - \delta) + 2\delta$$
$$4 \ge 5 - 5\delta + 2\delta$$
$$4 \ge 5 - 3\delta$$
$$3\delta \ge 1 \iff \mathbf{\delta \ge \frac{1}{3}}$$

> **Economic Meaning of $\delta \ge \frac{1}{3}$:**  
> As long as firms discount the future by less than $66.7\%$ per period (or probability of continuing exceeds $33.3\%$), the threat of future margin collapse from $4$ down to $2$ completely overcomes the one-time temptation to pocket an extra $5 - 4 = 1$.

---

### Derivation 4: General Grim Trigger Formula

Let:
- $R$ = Reward for mutual cooperation (e.g. $4$)
- $T$ = Temptation payoff from unilateral deviation (e.g. $5$)
- $P$ = Punishment payoff under punishment equilibrium (e.g. $2$)
- $S$ = Sucker's payoff when cheated (e.g. $1$)

The incentive constraint:
$$\frac{R}{1 - \delta} \ge T + \frac{\delta P}{1 - \delta} \iff R \ge T(1 - \delta) + \delta P \iff \delta(T - P) \ge T - R$$

$$\mathbf{\delta^* = \frac{T - R}{T - P}}$$

#### Strategic Sensitivities:
1. **Temptation Gain ($T - R$):** As the short-term prize from cheating increases, $\delta^*$ rises (cooperation becomes harder).
2. **Punishment Severity ($R - P$):** As the punishment payoff $P$ decreases (harsher retaliatory pain), $\delta^*$ drops (cooperation becomes easier).

---

### Derivation 5: Asymmetric Relationships & Dual Thresholds

In many supply chain partnerships, one firm is a dominant buyer and the other is a small supplier:

$$\delta_1^* = \frac{T_1 - R_1}{T_1 - P_1}, \qquad \delta_2^* = \frac{T_2 - R_2}{T_2 - P_2}$$

$$\mathbf{\text{Binding Condition: } \delta \ge \max(\delta_1^*, \delta_2^*)}$$

Both parties must be sufficiently patient. The player with the larger temptation gain or milder punishment payoff is the **weakest link** whose threshold dictates whether the partnership survives.

---

### Derivation 6: Tit-for-Tat Alternating Retaliation

If a firm deviates against exact Tit for Tat, play alternates between $[D, C]$ and $[C, D]$:
Payoff sequence: $5, 1, 5, 1, \dots$

$$V_{TFT} = 5 + \delta + 5\delta^2 + \delta^3 + \dots = \frac{5 + \delta}{1 - \delta^2}$$

Comparing $\frac{4}{1 - \delta} \ge \frac{5 + \delta}{1 - \delta^2} \implies \frac{4(1 + \delta)}{1 - \delta^2} \ge \frac{5 + \delta}{1 - \delta^2} \implies 4 + 4\delta \ge 5 + \delta \implies 3\delta \ge 1 \implies \mathbf{\delta \ge \frac{1}{3}}$.

---

## 9. Practical Realities: Monitoring & Recovery

In theoretical models, actions are observed with $100\%$ precision without delay. In executive reality:

1. **The Monitoring Problem:**
   - Secret volume rebates, non-price perks, and customized enterprise bundling mask cheating.
   - External market downturns (e.g., industry demand drop) look indistinguishable from competitor price cuts.
2. **The Recovery Problem:**
   - Permanent Grim Trigger punishment destroys trillions in enterprise value after a simple false positive.
   - Sustainable commercial alliances require **monitoring conventions** (industry transparency indices, audits) and **forgiving reset mechanisms** (e.g., Contrite Tit-for-Tat, finite punishment phases).

## 10. Common Executive Pitfalls to Avoid

| Common Mistake | Executive Correction |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Treating repeated play as independent one-shot rounds** | Explicitly define how tomorrow's action depends on yesterday's history. |
| **Ignoring the known contract expiration date** | Anticipate end-of-contract unraveling; restructure contracts with automatic renewals or performance earnouts. |
| **Adding future profits without discounting** | Weight period $t$ payoffs by $\delta^t$; cash today is not cash in 5 years. |
| **Checking only one player in asymmetric alliances** | Always compute separate thresholds for both buyer and supplier. |
| **Assuming punishment is automatic and costless** | Ensure retaliatory threats are observable, attributable, and credible to execute. |

## 2. Opening Executive Decision: A Licence Worth HK$100,000

Consider an opening dilemma: Your firm and a competitor submit sealed bids for an exclusive commercial operating licence. Your corporate valuation of the licence is **HK$100,000**. You do not know your rival's valuation or planned bid.

- **Rule A (First-Price):** Highest bid wins; winner pays their **own bid**.
- **Rule B (Second-Price):** Highest bid wins; winner pays the **highest losing bid** (second price).

### Key Insight:
Should you submit the exact same bid under both rules? **No!**
- Under Rule A, bidding HK$100,000 guarantees **zero profit** even if you win ($100,000 - 100,000 = 0$). You must strategically **shade your bid** below HK$100,000.
- Under Rule B, your bid decides only **whether you win**, not **what you pay**. Bidding your exact valuation of HK$100,000 is a **weakly dominant strategy**.

## 3. Valuation Environments: Private Value vs. Common Value

Before analyzing bidding rules, an executive must classify the **informational environment**:

| Environment | Economic Definition | Corporate Examples | Strategic Question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Private Value** | A bidder's valuation depends exclusively on its own internal preferences, cost structure, or end-use utility. Learning rivals' valuations would not alter own value. | Art acquired for personal enjoyment, custom manufacturing equipment, proprietary internal software licenses. | *"What is this asset worth to my specific business?"* |
| **Common Value** | The underlying asset possesses a shared, objective monetary value across all competitors, but bidders hold imperfect, noisy private signals or engineering estimates. | Offshore oil drilling blocks, company acquisition for resale, 5G wireless spectrum rights, government mineral extraction. | *"Would learning rivals' signals change my estimate of the asset's true value?"* |

## 4. The Four Canonical Auction Formats

Every auction mechanism consists of an **allocation rule** and a **payment rule**:

```
                       ┌───────────────────────────────┐
                       │    Canonical Auction Formats  │
                       └──────────────┬────────────────┘
                                      │
            ┌─────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┐
            ▼                                                   ▼
     [ Sealed Bids ]                                     [ Open Formats ]
   ┌────────────────────────────────┐                  ┌────────────────────────────────┐
   │ 1. First-Price Sealed Bid      │                  │ 3. English Ascending           │
   │    Highest bid wins, pays own  │                  │    Price rises until 1 remains │
   │ 2. Second-Price Sealed (Vickrey│                  │ 4. Dutch Descending Clock      │
   │    Highest bid wins, pays 2nd  │                  │    Price drops until 1 accepts │
   └────────────────────────────────┘                  └────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Strategic Equivalence Pairs:
1. **English Open Ascending $\approx$ Second-Price Sealed Bid (Vickrey):**  
   In an English ascending auction with private values, each bidder remains active until the standing price reaches their valuation. The auction terminates the instant the second-highest bidder drops out. The winner acquires the asset at approximately the second-highest valuation!
2. **Dutch Open Descending $\approx$ First-Price Sealed Bid:**  
   In a Dutch clock auction, no information is revealed while the clock ticks downward. The first bidder to hit the buzzer wins and immediately pays that standing clock price. Deciding when to stop the clock requires balancing the exact same trade-off as choosing a sealed bid in a first-price auction.

## 5. Second-Price Auction: Why Truthful Bidding ($b=v$) is Weakly Dominant

In a private-value Second-Price (Vickrey) auction, **bidding your true valuation ($b^* = v$) is a weakly dominant strategy**. It yields a payoff at least as high as any alternative bid, regardless of the highest rival bid $r$.

### Mathematical Dominance Proof:
Let your true valuation be $v$, your submitted bid be $b$, and the highest rival bid be $r$:

1. **Scenario 1: Highest Rival Bid Below Your Value ($r < v$):**
   - You want to win because the transaction is profitable: Surplus $= v - r > 0$.
   - **Bidding $b = v$:** Wins and pays $r$, yielding positive surplus $v - r > 0$.
   - **Underbidding ($b < v$):** If you underbid below $r$ ($b < r < v$), you **lose** the auction, earning $0$ instead of $v - r$. If you underbid above $r$ ($r < b < v$), you still pay $r$. Underbidding never reduces your payment; it only creates the risk of losing profitable deals!

2. **Scenario 2: Highest Rival Bid Above Your Value ($r > v$):**
   - You want to lose because winning requires paying more than the item is worth: $v - r < 0$.
   - **Bidding $b = v$:** Loses cleanly and pays nothing, yielding surplus $0$.
   - **Overbidding ($b > v$):** If you overbid above $r$ ($v < r < b$), you win the item but must pay $r > v$, suffering a **net loss** ($v - r < 0$). If you overbid below $r$ ($v < b < r$), you still lose. Overbidding never improves your surplus; it only introduces the catastrophic risk of winning at an unprofitable price!

> **The Vickrey Separation Principle:**  
> *Your bid determines whether you win; the rival's bid determines what you pay.*

## 6. First-Price Auction: The Bid-Shading Optimization Trade-off

In a First-Price auction, the winner pays their **own bid**. Bidding your true value ($b = v$) guarantees zero surplus upon winning ($v - v = 0$). Bidders must strategically **shade their bids**.

### The Fundamental Trade-off:
- **Bid Higher ($b \uparrow$):** Increases the probability of winning $\Pr(\text{Win}) = F(b)^{n-1}$, but leaves a smaller profit margin conditional on winning $(v - b)$.
- **Bid Lower ($b \downarrow$):** Captures a larger profit margin $(v - b)$ if you win, but increases the probability that a rival outbids you.

### Risk-Neutral Symmetric Bayesian Nash Equilibrium:
Under $n$ symmetric, risk-neutral bidders with private valuations distributed uniformly on $[0, V_{\max}]$:

$$b^*(v) = \frac{n - 1}{n} v$$

| Number of Bidders ($n$) | Equilibrium Bid $b^*(v)$ | Strategic Interpretation |
| :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **$n = 2$** | $b^*(v) = \frac{1}{2} v = 0.50 v$ | Heavy shading; low competitive pressure allows capturing 50% margin. |
| **$n = 3$** | $b^*(v) = \frac{2}{3} v \approx 0.67 v$ | Moderate shading; rival competition disciplines discounts. |
| **$n = 4$** | $b^*(v) = \frac{3}{4} v = 0.75 v$ | High competition; bidders bid 75% of value. |
| **$n \to \infty$** | $b^*(v) \to v$ | Perfectly competitive limit; bid shading vanishes. |

### The Impact of Risk Aversion:
If bidders are **risk-averse** (represented by utility $u(w) = w^\alpha$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$):
$$b^*(v) = \frac{n - 1}{n - 1 + \alpha} v > \frac{n - 1}{n} v$$
Risk-averse executives **shade less aggressively** (bid higher) because the fear of losing entirely outweighs the desire for extra profit margin.

## 7. The Revenue Equivalence Theorem: Benchmark & Caveats

### The Theorem (Vickrey 1961, Riley & Samuelson 1981):
> Under four benchmark conditions, **all four canonical auction formats** (First-Price, Second-Price, English, Dutch) yield the **exact same expected seller revenue**!

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{Seller Revenue}] = \frac{n - 1}{n + 1} V_{\max}$$

### Intuition:
Suppose four bidders value an asset at 10, 9, 8, and 7.
- In a Second-Price auction, truthful bidding causes the bidder with valuation 10 to win and pay **9**.
- In a First-Price auction, the winner pays their own bid, but shades it. Under equilibrium shading, the expected bid of the highest-valuation bidder equals **9**.
Strategic bid adjustment perfectly offsets the apparent difference in payment rules on average!

### The 4 Strict Assumptions (When Revenue Equivalence Breaks):
1. **Risk Neutrality:** If bidders are risk-averse, First-Price auctions yield strictly **higher revenue** than Second-Price auctions because fear of losing forces aggressive bidding.
2. **Symmetric Bidders:** If one bidder has a known structural advantage (e.g., an incumbent vs. an entrant), Second-Price auctions can suffer from entrant deterrence, while First-Price auctions may generate higher revenue.
3. **Independent Private Values (IPV):** In common-value settings, English auctions reveal rivals' signals dynamically, mitigating the Winner's Curse and yielding higher seller revenue than sealed-bid formats.
4. **Efficient Allocation to the Highest Valuation:** Reserve prices, bidder caps, and entry fees distort allocations.

## 8. The Winner's Curse in Common-Value Settings

In common-value auctions (e.g., mineral rights, real estate redevelopment, tech acquisitions), the asset has a shared true value $V$, but each bidder receives an imperfect private signal $s_i = V + \epsilon_i$.

### The Core Information Trap:
- If all bidders submit bids based on their naive unconditional estimate $\mathbb{E}[V | s_i]$, **the winner is the bidder who received the highest, most optimistic error $\max(\epsilon_i)$**!
- **Winning is informative:** The very fact that you won means that *every single competitor estimated the asset to be worth less than you did*.
- If you failed to discount for this adverse selection in advance, you will overpay:

$$\mathbb{E}[V \mid \text{Winning}] = \mathbb{E}[V \mid s_i > \max_{j \neq i} s_j] < \mathbb{E}[V \mid s_i]$$

> **Executive Rule:**  
> *Rational bidding requires conditioning your bid not on your unconditional estimate today, but on the hypothetical scenario that your estimate turns out to be the highest among all competitors.*

## 9. Modern Strategic Applications

### 1. Platform Proxy Bidding (eBay Style):
Bidders enter their maximum willingness to pay ($v$). The automated proxy agent raises the standing bid by the minimum required increment whenever a competitor bids, stopping strictly at the stated ceiling. This operationalizes an English ascending auction with minimal monitoring costs.

### 2. Digital Search Advertising (Google Ads & GSP):
Search engines allocate sponsored ad positions using **Generalized Second Price (GSP)** auctions. Advertisers submit maximum bids per click, which are multiplied by a Quality Score (CTR and relevance). The winner pays the minimum bid necessary to defeat the advertiser ranked directly below them.

### 3. Spectrum Auctions:
Governments allocate radio spectrum blocks characterized by **complementarities** (neighbouring geographic regions are more valuable together) and **substitutabilities** (overlapping frequencies cause technical redundancy). Designing spectrum auctions requires combinatorial clock package mechanics.

### 4. Procurement & Reverse Auctions:
In corporate procurement, one buyer solicits bids from multiple suppliers. The lowest bidder wins.
- **Two-Dimensional Scoring:** Because low-cost suppliers often carry higher bankruptcy, delivery, or quality failure risks, enterprise buyers deploy formulaic scoring:
$$\text{Total Score} = w_{\text{price}} \cdot \text{PriceScore} + w_{\text{quality}} \cdot \text{QualityScore}$$

## 10. Checklist for Auction Strategy & Analysis (Slide 25)

When preparing to bid in or design an auction, execute this 6-step diagnostic checklist:

1. **Define the Environment:** What object or package is being allocated? Who are the eligible competitors?
2. **Classify Values:** Is this a private-value setting (own use/cost) or a common-value setting (shared resale/production asset)?
3. **Inspect the Rules:** Are bids open or sealed? What is the allocation rule? What is the payment rule (own bid vs. second price)?
4. **Check Information Flows:** What information is revealed before, during, and after bidding?
5. **Derive the Strategic Incentive:** Should you bid truthfully (Second-Price / English) or shade your bid (First-Price / Dutch)?
6. **Adjust for the Winner's Curse & Quality:** If common values exist, shade for adverse selection. If procurement, state verifiable minimum quality standards.

## 11. Exit Ticket Synthesis & Key Formulas

- **Second-Price Truthful Bidding:** $b^*(v) = v$ (Weakly dominant in private values).
- **First-Price Bid Shading:** $b^*(v) = \frac{n-1}{n} v$ (Under risk neutrality and uniform $[0, V_{\max}]$).
- **Revenue Equivalence Benchmark:** $\mathbb{E}[\text{Revenue}] = \frac{n-1}{n+1} V_{\max}$ across all four canonical formats under IPV.
- **Winner's Curse Correction:** $\text{Bid} = \mathbb{E}[V \mid s_i = \max_{j} s_j]$.
