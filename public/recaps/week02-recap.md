# Course Recap: Sequential Games and Backward Induction

The second class reviewed strategic interdependence, dominant strategies, and the prisoner’s dilemma before introducing **sequential games**, in which players move in a particular order. The main focus was how the timing of decisions affects strategic behavior and how players can anticipate future responses when making current decisions.

### Review of Strategic Interdependence

The class began by revisiting the idea that a player’s best decision depends on what other players are expected to do. Examples such as price competition illustrated how individually rational choices can produce poor outcomes for everyone.

Although cooperation may improve the collective outcome, it can be difficult to sustain when each player has an incentive to act in their own short-term interest. Trust, shared norms, repeated interaction, and changes in incentives may help players avoid these outcomes.

### Simultaneous and Sequential Games

The class distinguished between two types of games:

- **Simultaneous games:** Players choose their actions without observing the current choices of others.
- **Sequential games:** Players move in turn, and later players observe earlier actions before making their decisions.

In a sequential game, the order of moves matters because an earlier action can influence how another player responds. Players must therefore look ahead and anticipate the consequences of each possible action.

Business examples included product launches, market entry, pricing decisions, lending rates, and production decisions. These examples demonstrated that the value of moving first or waiting depends on the structure of the particular game.

### Representing Sequential Games with Game Trees

Sequential games can be represented using a **game tree**. A game tree identifies:

- the players;
- the order in which they move;
- the actions available at each decision point;
- the possible paths generated by those actions; and
- the payoffs at the end of each path.

Each decision point is represented by a node, while the branches represent the available actions. A finite game tree has a clear beginning and ends at terminal nodes, where the players’ payoffs are specified.

Constructing the game tree requires careful judgment about who moves first, what later players observe, and which actions are realistically available. An incorrectly specified sequence of moves can lead to an incorrect strategic prediction.

### Actions and Strategies

The class also distinguished between an **action** and a **strategy**. An action is a particular choice made at one decision point. A strategy is a complete plan describing what a player would do at every decision point at which they might be called upon to act.

When a player moves only once, the distinction between an action and a strategy may not appear important. When a player may move more than once or may need to respond to different earlier actions, however, a complete strategy must specify the player’s intended response in every possible situation.

### Backward Induction

The main analytical technique introduced in the class was **backward induction**. Rather than beginning at the start of the game and simply predicting what might happen next, backward induction begins with the final decision.

The method involves three steps:

1. Identify the last player required to make a decision.
2. Determine which action that player would choose at each possible final decision point.
3. Work backward through the game tree, using these predicted choices to determine what earlier players should do.

By anticipating how later players will respond, an earlier player can select the action that produces the most favorable eventual outcome. Backward induction therefore connects present decisions with their future strategic consequences.

### Market Entry and the Incumbent’s Response

Market entry was used as a central example. A potential entrant first decides whether to enter a market. If entry occurs, the incumbent firm must decide whether to **fight** the entrant or **accommodate** its entry.

The entrant should not base its decision only on the incumbent’s stated threat to fight. Instead, it should examine the incumbent’s payoffs after entry has already occurred. If accommodating entry would be more profitable than fighting, a rational incumbent would accommodate at that stage.

The entrant can anticipate this response through backward induction. If entry followed by accommodation gives the entrant a favorable payoff, it may choose to enter despite the incumbent’s threat.

This example shows that strategic decisions should be based on what another player will have an incentive to do when the relevant decision point is reached, rather than simply on what that player currently claims it will do.

### Credible and Non-Credible Threats

The market-entry example introduced the importance of **credibility**. A threat can influence another player’s behavior only if carrying it out would be rational when the time comes.

An incumbent may announce that it will respond aggressively to any entrant. However, if fighting is costly and accommodation becomes more profitable after entry, the threat may not be credible. A rational entrant should therefore distinguish between the incumbent’s words and its actual future incentives.

A threat becomes more credible when the player has made a commitment that changes those incentives or makes retreat difficult. Investments in excess capacity, contractual obligations, established policies, and reputational concerns may make a threatened response more believable.

### First-Mover and Second-Mover Advantages

The class examined whether moving first creates a strategic advantage. A first mover may shape the game by making an early commitment, securing customers or resources, or limiting the choices of later players.

However, moving first is not always better. A later mover may observe the first mover’s action, learn from its experience, and adjust accordingly. Waiting may allow a firm to avoid early mistakes, improve its product, or respond more effectively to market conditions.

Whether there is a **first-mover advantage** or **second-mover advantage** therefore depends on the specific payoffs, information, and available responses in the game.

### Technology Launch Timing

The timing of a technology launch provided an application of sequential-game analysis. A firm may choose to launch early or wait until competitors reveal their products and the market provides more information.

Launching early may allow a firm to:

- establish a market position;
- attract users before competitors;
- build a reputation as an innovator; and
- influence subsequent industry development.

Waiting may allow a firm to:

- observe the performance of an early entrant;
- learn from its successes and failures;
- improve the technology before launching; and
- avoid some of the uncertainty and cost of pioneering a new market.

The appropriate choice depends on how competitors are expected to respond and how the benefits of early entry compare with the informational advantages of waiting.

### Platform Entry and Regulatory Responses

The class also applied game-tree analysis to decisions involving platforms and regulators. A platform might choose among entry methods such as organic expansion, acquisition, or partnership, after which a regulator may approve, restrict, or challenge the chosen approach.

To analyze such a situation, the model must clearly identify:

- the platform and regulator as players;
- the sequence of their decisions;
- the entry methods available to the platform;
- the possible regulatory responses; and
- the payoffs associated with each final outcome.

The platform should anticipate how the regulator is likely to respond to each entry method. This expected regulatory response then influences the platform’s initial choice.

### Practical Role of Economic Models

The class emphasized that game trees are simplified representations of business situations. Their purpose is not to reproduce every detail of reality, but to isolate the most important strategic relationships.

A useful model should identify the relevant players, actions, timing, information, and payoffs clearly enough to explain the logic behind a decision. Assumptions and payoff assignments should be justified because different assumptions may generate different predicted outcomes.

## Overall Academic Takeaway

The central lesson of the second class was that **timing changes strategic incentives**. In a sequential game, a player should anticipate how later players will respond before selecting an initial action.

Backward induction provides a systematic method for doing this. It begins with the final decisions, determines what rational players would do at those points, and then works backward to identify the optimal earlier choices.

The analysis also demonstrated that threats are strategically relevant only when they are credible and that moving first is not automatically advantageous. The value of moving early, waiting, committing, fighting, or accommodating depends on the structure and payoffs of the particular game.
